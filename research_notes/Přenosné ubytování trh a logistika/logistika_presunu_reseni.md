## 1. Baseline: assembled container size and weight, units per truck, crane needs, Czech costs, and moving a 100-bed camp

### Takeaway
A 20′ office or accommodation container is light (about 1.6–2.9 t) but bulky (about 6.06 × 2.44 × 2.59–2.80 m). A truck runs out of space long before it reaches its weight limit: it carries 1 unit (solo HDS truck) or 2 units (HDS truck with trailer, or a semi) and uses only about 10–15% of its gross-weight allowance. Every move also needs lifting at both ends (HDS, mobile crane, or a special self-loader). Published Czech rates (35–65 Kč/km plus about 800 Kč/h of handling) put pure trucking for a 100-bed camp at about **17–31 truckloads and about 150–480 k Kč per 100 km move**, before any site works.

### Inferences
- **Space, not weight, limits the truck.** Two 20′ units take about 12.1 m of a standard 13.6 m semi deck (the deck length is my assumption) and weigh only 3.2–5.7 t. The gross limit for a 5-axle combination is 40 t (Section 2). Stacking assembled units is impossible: two units are about 5.2 m tall before adding the deck, against a 4.00 m height limit.
- **Height is tight even for one unit.** A 2.80 m CLASSIC unit on a flatbed with a deck of about 1.2–1.3 m (unsourced typical value) reaches about 4.0–4.1 m. That means low-deck trailers, which narrows the pool of usable trucks.
- **HDS lifting capacity is a hidden constraint.** A 2.4 t HDS covers BASIC and CLASSIC units (1.6–1.95 t) but not the XL (2.85 t) or, most likely, a plumbed sanitary unit. Heavier units need a larger HDS or a mobile crane, which is a step change in cost. I found no sanitary-unit weights.
- **Fixed cost per load dominates.** The Skanska rates work out to about 19 Kč/km per container when two travel together, against 35 Kč/km for one. The main lever is putting more boxes or more m² on each truck, not finding cheaper kilometres.
- **[Calc] Moving a 100-bed camp 100 km (my assumptions, not sourced):**
  - Units: 2–4 beds per 20′ sleeping unit gives 25–50 sleeping units. Adding about 8–12 ancillary units (sanitary, kitchen and dining, office, laundry) gives **33–62 units**.
  - Truckloads: at 2 units per load, **17–31 truckloads**.
  - Cost per load: billed both ways (200 km at 35–65 Kč/km) = 7,000–13,000 Kč. Handling of 2–3 h at 800 Kč/h = 1,600–2,400 Kč. Total about 8,600–15,400 Kč per load.
  - Total: **about 146–477 k Kč per move, about 1,500–4,800 Kč per bed** (about €6–19 k, or €58–191 per bed, at an assumed 25 Kč/€).
  - Excluded: tolls, crane hire for heavy units, oversize permits for XL units, and site works (levelling and foundations, stairs, interconnecting cabling and plumbing, reconnection and inspections).
  - Time: with 3–4 HDS trucks each doing about 2 loads a day at 100 km, trucking takes **about 2–5 working days**. A full camp turnaround, from disconnecting to reconnecting and approval, is plausibly 1–3 weeks. This is unverified and needs carrier quotes.
- The Ausco "about 2 days" claim shows that **plug-and-play utilities and pre-planned layouts** shorten set-up as much as the transport method does.

## 2. EU road limits (Directive 96/53/EC) and Czech oversize transport (nadrozměrná přeprava)

### Takeaway
EU limits cap vehicle width at **2.55 m** and height at **4.00 m**. Any module wider than 2.55 m in transport (3 m XL containers, 3–4 m mobilheims) therefore needs a Czech *povolení zvláštního užívání*. Since **1 March 2024**, businesses can apply only electronically through **IS NADR**, and repeat permits can last up to a year. The notified draft rule requires **1 escort vehicle above 3.00 m width, 2 above 3.50 m, and 3 above 4.20 m**. Older guidance quotes fees **from 1,200 Kč** and decisions in up to 30–60 days for non-simple cases.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Directive 96/53/EC, Annex I:
  - Maximum width 2.55 m (2.60 m for temperature-controlled bodies).
  - Maximum height 4.00 m.
  - Maximum length 12.00 m for a motor vehicle or trailer, **16.50 m for an articulated vehicle** and **18.75 m for a road train**.
  — [Directive 96/53/EC Annex I (legislation.gov.uk copy)](https://legislation.gov.uk/eudr/1996/53/annexes/data.xht); [EUR-Lex consolidated text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/1996/53/2002-03-09/eng)
- [Official, secondary] Germany allows 40 t for road trains and articulated vehicles with 5 or more axles. The limit can be raised for alternative-fuel or zero-emission vehicles. — [ITF/OECD weights and dimensions: Germany](https://www.itf-oecd.org/road-transport-group/weights-and-dimensions/germany)
- [Official] The Commission proposed amending 96/53/EC in COM(2023) 445. — [Council document ST-11722-2023-ADD-1](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-11722-2023-ADD-1/en/pdf)

- [Official] Related e-services: the permit for special use of a road ([portal.gov.cz S67985](https://portal.gov.cz/sluzby-vs/povoleni-zvlastniho-uzivani-pozemni-komunikace-S67985)), and a separate MD exemption from national truck driving bans ([portal.gov.cz S551](https://portal.gov.cz/sluzby-vs/vyjimka-z-omezeni-jizdy-nekterych-vozidel-v-ramci-cele-cr-S551)).
- [Forum] A 4 m-wide trailer such as a mobilheim counts as oversize transport and needs a special permit, for a fee. — [poradte.cz](https://www.poradte.cz/auto-moto/40965-nadmerny-prives-za-auto-administrativa.html)

### Inferences
- **2.55 m is the binding design rule.** A module that is 2.55 m or narrower in transport mode, natively or when folded, needs no permit, no escort and no route planning. Every relocation then becomes an ordinary truck job.
- **About 3.0 m is a middle ground.** CONTAINEX XL units (2,989 mm) need a permit on every move but stay under the 3.00 m escort threshold in the draft table. That buys about 23% more floor width per unit at the cost of paperwork and IS NADR lead time.
- **4 m mobilheims or double-width modules are expensive to move.** Under the draft table a 4.0 m load needs a permit **and 2 escort vehicles**, plus possible police conditions. This makes them unsuitable for frequent relocation.
- Repeat permits valid for a year help a fleet operator who moves the same oversize module type on recurring routes. Ad-hoc crisis moves still face the 30–60-day decision window unless the case counts as "simple".

### Gaps
- Current (post-1 March 2024) fee amounts, the actual statutory decision deadlines after the amendment, and whether the escort table notified under TRIS 26193 was adopted unchanged. I could not fetch the MD PDF.
- Market prices for escort vehicles (doprovodné vozidlo) per hour or per km in CZ, and how often police escort is required in practice.
- Whether COM(2023) 445 (the revision of 96/53/EC) had been adopted or changed width and height limits by Oct 2026.
- Czech national weight limits, including whether Czechia allows more than 40 t for some combinations. I did not verify these.

## 3. Shipping less empty space: flat-pack, folding and expandable units

### Takeaway
Shrinking the box in transport attacks the space problem directly:
- **Flat-pack** 20′ units: vendors claim **4–8 units per 40′ HQ** shipping container (against 2 assembled units per road semi), with **1–4 h assembly per unit using 2–4 workers**.
- **Folding** units: Continest claims **20 folded CN10 units per standard truck, or 24 on a mega trailer**, at **900 kg** each.
- **Self-deploying** products are fast but immature or not available in the EU. Ten Fold (UK) unfolds in 8–10 min to about 3× its transport size and has no visible commercial activity since about 2022. Boxabl Casita (US) unfolds in about 1 h, but folds to 2.59 m, above the EU's 2.55 m limit.
- **Expandable double-wing** 20′ units give **about 2.8× floor area per transport footprint** but still need lifting.

### Inferences
- **Truckload ratios versus the assembled baseline of 2 per semi:**
  - Flat-pack: about 2–4× fewer truckloads. This takes the 40′ HQ counts (4–8) as a stand-in for a 13.6 m trailer, which is my assumption.
  - Continest-type folding: up to about 10× by unit count (20 against 2). The model name suggests CN10 is a 10-ft unit (unverified), so by floor area the gain is more likely about 4–5×.
  - Expandable: about 2.5× more floor area per load (2 × 37.3 m² against 2 × 14.8 m²).
  - Boxabl: about 2.2× (15.8 m² folded footprint to 34.8 m²).
  - Ten Fold: about 3×.
- **[Calc] The same 100-bed camp (33–62 units) in flat-pack form:**
  - Truckloads: **about 5–16** instead of 17–31, roughly 50–70% fewer.
  - Labour: on vendor ranges, assembly takes **about 66 person-hours** (33 units × 1 h × 2 workers) to **about 990 person-hours** (62 × 4 h × 4 workers), and teardown adds a similar order.
  - Flat-pack therefore **trades trucking cost for assembly labour**. It pays off on long hauls and storage (depots hold several times more units per m²). On short hops with expensive labour it may not.
- **Folding beats flat-pack on labour.** It needs minutes to hours and few or no loose parts. But durability over repeated fold cycles, weather sealing at the hinges and EU compliance are unproven in the sources found.
- **Boxabl does not fit EU limits as shipped.** At 2.59 m folded it exceeds 2.55 m, so EU moves would need permits. No EU certification was found.
- **Comfort parity is not automatic.** Flat-pack and expandable units are panel-built, and the insulation, fire class and acoustic performance of joints and wings must be checked against Czech requirements. I found no data.

## 4. Moving without a crane: hooklift (Abroll) modules, sidelifters, container jacks, and units on their own chassis

### Takeaway
Mature crane-free handling methods exist:
- **Hooklift (Abroll) bodies** to DIN 30722, 4–7 m long. Czech waste hauliers charge about 65 Kč/km and only about 100 Kč per load or unload event.
- **Sidelifters**, with payloads up to 36 t.
- **Portable container jacks** in sets of 4, rated 4–15 t, stocked in MSF's catalogue.
- **Wheeled units**: Bauwagen limited to 25 km/h, or 80/100 km/h versions under about 1.5 t; tiny houses within 2.55 m width, 4 m height and 3.5 t in Germany.

These methods cut crane dependence, crew and handling time, but **not the space problem**: each still moves 1–2 boxes per truck. German fire services already field hooklift accommodation pods ("AB Aufenthalt", about €125k each, with WC, kitchenette and independent power), which proves the concept, but without beds.

### Inferences
- **Hooklift accommodation pods fit Czech conditions best.** Hooklift trucks are standard equipment in Czech waste and municipal haulage, as the Czech-language hooklift tests and price lists show. Loading and unloading become a one-driver operation, and ASOMPO charges just 100 Kč per load or unload event against about 800 Kč/h for HDS handling at Skanska.
  - Constraints: body length up to about 7 m (DIN 30722) and width up to 2.55 m. Body height is limited to roughly 2.6–2.7 m so the total stays under 4.0 m on the truck. That limit is my estimate and is unverified.
  - The hook frame and rails add weight and cost, and the pod must tolerate being tilted during loading, which matters for sanitary fittings and furniture fixings.
  - No Czech accommodation-grade hooklift product was found. That looks like a **product gap and an opportunity**, unverified.
- **Sidelifters** keep standard ISO-cornered units (most accommodation containers) and remove the crane at both ends. They need side clearance and have no payload problem with 2–3 t units. Czech availability is unknown.
- **Jacks** are the cheapest crane-free option: a set of 4 travels with the truck and works on any flatbed. They are slower and need firm, level ground. They suit remote, low-frequency moves and humanitarian use, as MSF practice shows.
- **Wheeled units** (Bauwagen, tiny houses) need no lifting and almost no set-up, but carry only about 1–2 beds per unit (my assumption). A 100-bed camp would need about 50–100 tows, so they suit small, frequently moving crews (linear works) rather than camps. Mobilheims over 3 m wide move into permit-and-escort territory (Section 2).
- None of these methods reduces the number of truckloads. Combined with Section 3 (fewer, denser loads) they address both halves of the founder's problem.

- Commercial Abroll or hooklift accommodation or sanitary modules sold in DE, AT or CZ (THW, military, rental), with bed counts, weights and prices. The fire-service AB Aufenthalt has no beds, and its €125k reflects fire-service specification.

## 5. Other transport modes: rail accommodation trains, floating accommodation, intermodal modules

### Takeaway
I found **no current documentation** of accommodation trains (Wohnzug, Unterkunftswagen) at Spitzke, Leonhard Weiss or Rhomberg Sersa, nor of Czech ubytovací vozy at SŽ or ČD. The only evidence is historical: GDR conversions in 1949, and a 2004 documentary about a 600 m track-renewal train that served as a "home on wheels" for about 40 workers. Floating accommodation is proven at scale for asylum housing: Bibby Stockholm in the UK (500 capacity; set-up £14.5m plus running £20.3m over 2 years) and Silja Europa in Velsen, NL (1,000–1,200 places). Its cost-effectiveness is contested, and it matters little for landlocked Czechia.

### Inferences
- **[Calc] Bibby Stockholm cost per person-night.** £34.8m over 730 days comes to about **£95** at 500 beds, about **£111** at the planned 430, and about **£149** at 321. The Telegraph's £6,000 per head per month is about £197 a night. Occupancy risk dominates the economics of large single assets.
- **Floating accommodation** relocates 500–1,500 beds in one move. It needs a deep-water berth, utilities, safety certification and political acceptance. In Czechia the navigable Labe and Vltava sections make it marginal for workforce or crisis housing; Prague *botels* are hospitality, not deployable assets.
- **Rail accommodation** only makes sense for linear rail works with possession and siding access. Czech and DACH contractors appear to rely on road-based camps, hotels or Monteurzimmer instead. This rests on absence of evidence and is not confirmed.
- **Intermodal (rail plus last mile)** still needs lifting at the railhead and at the site, so it removes long-haul road kilometres but not the crane. It helps only for long distances (several hundred km) or cross-border deployments.

## 6. Fast-deploying soft structures: tensile, fabric, expandable and humanitarian flat-pack

### Takeaway
Soft and expandable structures deploy fastest and carry the most m² per kg, but the standard achieved varies widely:
- **Better Shelter**: 17.5 m² for 4–5 people, built by 4 people in about 4–8 h, packed in 2 boxes, life about 3 years.
- **Weatherhaven**: TRECC deploys in under 3 min with 2 people; MECC triples floor space and is operational in about 30 min.
- **Sprung**: insulated to R-25–R-47, erected at up to 186 m² per day, 2–3 weeks lead time.

Berlin's Tegel emergency site, built from lightweight halls and containers, shows operating costs of **€155–165 per place per day**, against about €35 in regular shared accommodation.

### Cited Findings
- **Better Shelter**:
  - [Database, updated Feb 2024] **17.5 m²** for a family of **4–5**. Built by **4 people in 5–6 h** without electricity, and needs at least 4 people. — [Engineering for Change](https://www.engineeringforchange.org/solutions/product/better-shelter)
  - [Press, 2015] Built by 4 people in **about 4 h** ([BGR](https://bgr.com/2015/03/26/ikea-better-shelter-modular-home/)), or by a team of 8 in **under 8 h** ([MacPlas](https://macplas.it/en/node/11872)).

### Inferences
- **What each type suits:**
  - Crisis surge (days to weeks): Better Shelter-class or tent halls. They are fastest and lightest but low-comfort, with no sanitary facilities built in.
  - Medium-term communal space (canteen, storage, common rooms): insulated tension-fabric buildings (Sprung-class, R-25 and above).
  - Military or premium rapid deployment: expandable containers (Weatherhaven).
- **Hybrid camp.** Keep sanitary and sleeping functions in hard modules, ideally folding or hooklift, and put dining, recreation and storage under fabric. That could remove a sizeable share of hard boxes from each relocation (my inference; not costed).
- **Fast build does not mean cheap to run.** Tegel's €155–165 per day shows a hastily built large emergency site can be very expensive per bed. Speed of deployment and cost per bed-night are separate metrics.

- Weight per Better Shelter unit, and per-m² prices for Sprung and Weatherhaven.
- Czech experience with tent cities for Ukrainian refugees in 2022 (costs, comfort complaints).

## 7. Network and operations: depots, repositioning, backhaul, pooling, telematics, and moving people instead of boxes

### Takeaway
Two operational levers are significant:
- **Empty running.** **21.8% of EU road-freight vehicle-km ran empty in 2023**, and domestic runs were worse (24% in 2020). Depot networks, pooling and backhaul matching target this directly.
- **Moving people instead of boxes.** Hinkley Point C housed workers in 1,496 campus beds and moved almost everyone by bus from three park-and-ride sites. It is proven at scale.

Cost-per-bed benchmarks (all-in operating costs, not relocation costs) range from **€12.5–35 per day** in regular German facilities to **€60–82 per day** in hostels and hotels, **€155–165 per day** at Tegel, and **about £95–197 per night** on the Bibby barge. I found no published cost per bed for relocation itself.

### Inferences
- **Depots.** Czech carriers price per km, typically including the return leg, at 35–65 Kč/km. A regional depot within about 50–100 km of demand clusters (for example Praha, Brno, Ostrava, Plzeň) cuts positioning and return kilometres for every move. Flat-pack or folded storage multiplies how many units a depot holds per m² (Section 3).
- **Backhaul and empty-leg matching.** With roughly 22% of EU truck-km running empty, a relocation could be timed to ride on a carrier's return leg, or the operator could pair a delivery with a collection in the same region. The likely saving per move is a sizeable share of the km cost (my inference; no Czech data).
- **Pooling and refurbishment.** The Aries 90-bed camp shows used-fleet reuse lowering cost. WillScot's 2025 numbers (units on rent falling while rates rose) suggest large fleets carry idle stock. A Czech operator could pool units across regions or clients and use **telematics** (GPS, door and usage sensors) to know where idle units sit before sending a truck. No telematics sources were found.
- **Moving people instead of boxes.**
  - For a short project or crisis with existing beds within about 45–60 min commuting (ubytovny, Monteurzimmer, hotels at off-season rates), shuttle buses plus existing beds can beat relocating a camp.
  - Hinkley shows the model at scale: campus beds plus park-and-ride with no on-site parking. Its £125m for 1,496 beds (**about £84k per bed**, my calculation) shows how capital-intensive fixed campuses are compared with renting existing beds.
  - The trade-off is ongoing travel time and cost against a one-off relocation (about 1.5–4.8 k Kč per bed per move for trucking alone, Section 1). Over a 6–12-month project, relocation is a small share of the total cost per bed. Over a 4–8-week job it is a large share.

### Gaps
- Telematics or fleet-tracking vendors and adoption among European modular lessors (Algeco, CONTAINEX, Touax); EU fleet utilisation rates.
- Czech ubytovny or Monteurzimmer prices per bed-night, and Czech shuttle-bus costs per seat-km, needed to quantify moving people instead of boxes.
- Any published cost per bed for relocating or remobilising a camp in the EU. None was found; the only per-bed figures are operating or capital costs.

## 8. Synthesis: which alternatives solve "moving containers by truck"? (comparison on common criteria)

### Takeaway
The pain point can be solved, but **by combining levers, not by any single product**:
1. **Fewer truckloads per bed**: flat-pack, folding or expandable units, at about 2–10× vendor-claimed density gains.
2. **No crane at either end**: hooklift-, sidelifter- or jack-compatible bases.
3. **Fewer empty and short-notice moves**: depots, pooling, backhaul, and moving people instead of boxes where beds already exist.

This research found no product that is EU-certified, crane-free and folds to 2.55 m or less. That intersection looks like white space, but this is unverified.

**What this means for the founder (my inference):**
1. **Biggest effect on cost per move: shrink the box.** Flat-pack or folding units at 2.55 m wide or less cut truckloads 2–10×. Folding avoids flat-pack's assembly labour. Due diligence should cover EU structural (EN 1090) and fire certification, joint and wing weather-tightness, and fold-cycle durability. None of these was verified here.
2. **Biggest effect on speed and crew: remove the crane.** A hooklift (DIN 30722) or sidelifter-compatible base, plus ISO corners for jacks, lets a single driver load and unload with the hooklift trucks already common in Czech waste haulage. No Czech supplier of hooklift accommodation pods was found, which may be a product opportunity.
3. **Design brief implied by the rules:**
   - Transport width 2.55 m or less: no permit, no escort.
   - Transport height about 2.6–2.7 m or less, to stay under 4.0 m on the deck (my assumption).
   - Length 7 m or less for a hooklift.
   - Weight within HDS or hooklift capacity (a 2.4 t HDS already excludes XL units).
   - Plug-and-play utility connections (Ausco reports about 2-day camp installs).
4. **Operations multiply the gains.** Regional depots, pooled fleets, backhaul matching (22% empty km in the EU) and telematics. For short jobs near existing beds, moving people instead of boxes (shuttle plus ubytovny) may beat any relocation.
5. **Niche modes are not a general fix for Czechia.** Floating and rail accommodation apply only in special cases. Soft shelters are for surges and communal space, not long-stay worker beds in winter.

### Gaps
- No independent, like-for-like cost comparison (Kč per bed per relocation) across these options. All per-unit transport and handling figures are vendor claims or price lists, and the camp composition is assumed.
- EU certification status (CE, EN 1090, EN 13501 fire, thermal) for every folding, flat-pack and expandable product named.
- Czech availability and prices for sidelifters, hooklift accommodation pods and container jacks.
- Several areas were not researched because the shared web-search budget ran out: ALGECO and Touax data, Losberger De Boer and Rubb data, Czech or German worker-accommodation space norms, and telematics vendors.
